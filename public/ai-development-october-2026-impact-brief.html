Field note 01 / Where AI helps—and where it hurts

Where AI helps—and where it hurts

The strongest evidence is not that AI makes every task better. It is that AI can produce substantial gains on some tasks and make performance worse on others.
Productivity gain
12.2%
Inside the tested capability frontier; outside it, AI assistance reduced correctness by 19%.

In a preregistered field experiment conducted with Boston Consulting Group, 758 knowledge workers completed realistic consulting tasks with or without GPT-4 assistance. On 18 tasks within the model’s capability frontier, AI-assisted workers completed 12.2% more tasks, completed them 25.1% faster, and produced higher-quality work. But on one complex managerial task selected to sit outside that frontier, AI-assisted workers were 19% less likely to produce a correct solution than workers without AI.

That is the operating lesson: AI is not a universal autopilot. It needs a task boundary, a quality check, and a clear stop condition.

The customer-support evidence points in the same direction. An NBER study of 5,179 customer-support agents found that access to a generative-AI conversational assistant increased issues resolved per hour by 14% on average and by 34% for novice and lower-skilled workers. The researchers also found improvements in customer sentiment and employee retention.

For an owner, the implication is practical: use AI to spread good working practices, reduce repetitive effort, and help newer staff get unstuck. Do not let a confident-looking answer bypass a review step simply because the first few results were good.

Sources:

  • Harvard Business School: Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier
  • NBER: Generative AI at Work

Field note 02 / A small-business AI ROI scorecard

A small-business AI ROI scorecard

Before buying a tool or building an automation, write down the baseline. “The team feels more productive” is useful feedback, but it is not yet an operating result.
ROI measures
6
Volume, cycle time, quality, human effort, business outcome, and risk.

Use a simple scorecard for one workflow:

MeasureBefore AIAfter AIWhat to watch
VolumeHow many items arrive?How many are processed?Demand may change during the test.
Cycle timeHow long from intake to completion?Same measure after rolloutSeparate waiting time from hands-on time.
QualityError, rework, or escalation rateSame definition after rolloutFaster is not better if corrections rise.
Human effortWho touches the work, and when?Which steps are assisted?Count review time, not just generation time.
Business outcomeCash collected, jobs closed, or customers retainedSame outcome after rolloutDo not substitute activity for value.
RiskWhat can go wrong?What guardrail catches it?Define the stop-and-review condition.

Run the scorecard on a narrow slice of work first. Keep the workflow stable long enough to compare like with like, and record exceptions rather than hiding them. The best first use case is usually repetitive, bounded, and easy for a person to verify.

Field note 03 / Trucking playbook: from paperwork to invoice to collection

Trucking playbook: from paperwork to invoice to collection

For a small carrier, the back office often has a recognizable chain:
Safe pattern
Suggest → approve
Evidence first, human approval for consequential commercial decisions.
  1. A proof of delivery, rate confirmation, and other load documents arrive.
  2. Someone reads the documents and matches them to the load.
  3. The invoice is prepared at the agreed rate.
  4. The invoice and supporting documents go to the customer or broker.
  5. Someone follows up until payment is received or an exception is resolved.

AI can assist at each handoff without owning the commercial decision. A document-extraction step can identify candidate fields and show the source page or image region. A matching step can flag that the reference number, delivery count, or rate does not agree. A drafting step can prepare an invoice or follow-up message for review. A collections queue can surface invoices that need attention.

The safe pattern is suggest, show evidence, approve, record. The system should preserve the original document, the extracted values, the person who approved them, and any correction. It should also route missing or conflicting information to a human instead of inventing a value.

Do not treat this as permission for automatic filing to any broker portal. Portal submission, rate exceptions, credits, disputes, and other external actions should remain explicitly approved until the carrier has a tested control process.

How to apply it: choose one document packet and one invoice type; define the required fields; add a confidence or exception rule; measure the workflow with the scorecard in Section 2; and review every output during the pilot.

Field note 04 / Trades playbook: from call to dispatch to job closeout

Trades playbook: from call to dispatch to job closeout

An HVAC, plumbing, electrical, or roofing business does not need an AI “employee” to get value from better workflow support. A bounded assistant can help turn an inbound call or message into a structured job request, summarize the customer’s issue, suggest the right service category, and prepare a technician-ready brief.
Workflow handoffs
4
Intake, dispatch support, technician support, and closeout.

The next handoffs are equally practical:

  • Intake: capture the customer’s words, location, urgency, and requested service.
  • Dispatch support: surface missing information and suggest a scheduling window for a dispatcher to approve.
  • Technician support: summarize the issue and attach relevant notes or photos.
  • Closeout: turn technician notes into a customer-ready summary, parts follow-up, and invoice-review checklist.

The human remains responsible for diagnosis, safety decisions, pricing, schedule commitments, and any promise made to the customer. AI can organize information; it should not silently turn an uncertain description into a definitive technical conclusion.

How to apply it: start with call summaries and job classification, not autonomous dispatch. Define the fields the dispatcher must confirm, keep an audit trail of edits, and test the workflow on ordinary jobs and messy edge cases such as incomplete addresses, emergency language, repeat visits, and conflicting notes.

Field note 05 / Professional offices: AI helps staff rather than replacing them

Professional offices: AI helps staff rather than replacing them

Professional work contains many tasks that are repetitive but still require judgment: summarizing a file, comparing documents, preparing a first draft, organizing research, or turning meeting notes into follow-up actions.
Reported flow
73%
Vendor-reported Copilot survey result; directional, not a revenue guarantee.

The BCG field experiment is useful here because it shows both sides of the equation. Inside the tested capability frontier, AI-assisted workers completed more tasks, completed them faster, and produced higher-quality solutions. Outside that frontier, performance could worsen. The right design is therefore an assistant that makes work easier to review—not a system that hides uncertainty behind polished prose.

GitHub’s research provides a second, narrower data point. In its survey of Copilot users, 73% reported staying in flow and 87% reported preserving mental effort during repetitive tasks. These are vendor-reported survey results, not independent measurements of revenue or cost savings, so they should be treated as directional evidence rather than a promise.

For an accounting, law, insurance, or real-estate office, the adoption sequence is straightforward:

  1. Start with search, summaries, first drafts, and checklists.
  2. Require the professional to verify citations, calculations, facts, and client-specific advice.
  3. Keep confidential data inside approved systems and define who can see prompts and outputs.
  4. Measure turnaround time and rework alongside client quality and staff experience.

How to apply it: pick one workflow where the professional already has a reliable review process. Make the source material visible, preserve the draft history, and give the reviewer a simple way to reject or correct the output.

Field note 06 / Short-term rentals (STRs): measure before you automate

Short-term rentals (STRs): measure before you automate

Short-term rentals and small hospitality operations have plenty of candidate workflows: guest-message drafting, listing-content updates, maintenance triage, cleaner coordination, review summarization, and booking follow-up.
Workflow first
1
Measure one property and one workflow before expanding automation.

But a polished message is not the same thing as a better property business. Before automating, separate the work into measurable steps:

  • How long does it take to answer a guest question?
  • How often does a message require a correction or escalation?
  • How many maintenance requests wait for a human response?
  • How often do cleaners or vendors need clarification?
  • Which booking or service outcomes are actually being tracked?

Start with assistance rather than autonomous guest-facing action. Let AI draft a response, identify the relevant house rule, or summarize a maintenance thread. Have the host approve anything involving refunds, safety, policy exceptions, sensitive guest information, or a commitment to a vendor.

How to apply it: choose one property and one workflow, capture a baseline, run a small assisted pilot, and compare quality and response time before expanding. If the operation cannot define the baseline, the next step is better measurement—not more automation.

## The small-business rule

Start with one job that repeats. Make the inputs visible. Define what a good output looks like. Keep a person responsible for the consequential decision. Then measure whether the work became faster, better, cheaper, or easier to staff.

AI is most useful when it makes a real process legible—and least useful when it is asked to hide the process.
